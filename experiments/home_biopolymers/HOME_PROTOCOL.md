# Practical prototype and test protocol

## Scope and first prototype

Start with **plain purchased PCL molding pellets**. The intended first test is non-food-contact water containment at **20–25°C**, a **5 cm water head**, and **24 hours**. This is a deliberately bounded test, not a universal waterproof claim. Food packaging, drinking-water use, hot-liquid containers and safety-critical parts need separate evidence and suitable certified materials.

The protocol below has not been performed in this study. The manufacturer supports plain-PCL forming; starch incorporation and the alginate/wax process are exploratory. No ingredient or finished article receives a food-contact or home-compost approval from this document.

Use a thermometer, a heat-resistant vessel, tongs or a spoon, a smooth mold, a balance readable to 0.01 g and, for comparison tests, a thickness gauge/caliper. Keep actual temperature, ingredient grade, batch size and observations in a copied [measurement template](results/measurements-template.csv). Make a simple part with continuous walls and no added seams for the initial PCL trial.

## A. Supported PCL forming route

1. Obtain an identified PCL molding product with its manufacturer's handling instructions. The nominal planning batch is **50 g plain PCL**. Record the actual weighed mass; grade additives and chain-end chemistry are not included in the calculation model.
2. Follow that product's instructions. The retrieved InstaMorph instructions specify **150°F / approximately 66°C water** and about **2 minutes** until the pellets soften and turn clear. The bath is hotter than comfortable skin contact. Use a thermometer and tools; the melting point itself is not a safe handling temperature.
3. Remove the softened mass with tongs or a spoon, drain it and allow it to cool enough for comfortable handling. Trapped hot water and the plastic itself can burn even after removal. Shape with tools while hot; do not put fingers into the bath or hot folds.
4. Mold a small cup or shallow vessel with enough wall height for the planned water head. Keep thickness reasonably uniform and record dimensions. Avoid joints, trapped bubbles and visibly thin spots. Also make a flat coupon for immersion measurements.
5. Let the article cool fully and become firm before filling it. Use supplier-approved hot-water reheating if reshaping is necessary. This protocol uses no solvents, direct-flame melting, oven compounding or polymer synthesis.
6. Record forming time, number of reheats, discarded/transferred material and defects. Record whether shaping was straightforward as an actual observation, rather than assuming that a manufacturer's plain-PCL method works for a modified blend.

The manufacturer warns that hot cars and dishwashers can soften the product. Keep this prototype to cool-water testing. Its FAQ also says that non-toxic handling does not mean certified food-safe. Water insolubility establishes neither leak-free molding nor zero vapor permeability.

## B. Optional PCL/native-starch discovery trials

These are **feed hypotheses**, not proven home-compounding recipes:

| Trial | Nominal PCL | Nominal dry starch | Role |
| --- | ---: | ---: | --- |
| PCL_S00 | 50 g | 0 g | Required plain-PCL control |
| PCL_S05 | 47.5 g | 2.5 g | Low-filler exploratory trial |
| PCL_S10 | 45 g | 5 g | Higher-filler exploratory trial |

Do the plain-PCL trial first. Native starch does not become thermoplastic just because PCL has softened. The retrieved blend papers use plasticized or modified starch and equipment/temperatures outside this home route. Water carried from the bath, native-powder moisture and poor interfacial adhesion may produce clumps, voids or leakage.

If exploring a filler trial, weigh the powder into a separate dry dish and record its as-received mass and unknown moisture. Introduce it only to the removed, drained, still-formable PCL using tools; do not pour powder into the water bath and then count all of it as incorporated. Keep the powder close to the dish and avoid creating airborne dust. Any subsequent hot-water reshaping may wash out filler, so record that loss as unknown unless measured. Stop a trial that cannot be worked into a continuous part within the supplier's forming route; do not increase temperature or add solvents to force a successful specimen. This is a process-feasibility experiment, not a validated blend procedure.

Uniform appearance alone does not prove microscopic dispersion. Test the resulting part against the matched plain-PCL control and record failures. No faster whole-polymer biodegradation, strength improvement or improved barrier performance is predicted from adding starch.

## C. Optional film made from ingredients

The alginate route is a comparator for someone interested in starting from simple film ingredients. It is **not the recommended water-holding prototype**. Calcium-treated alginate can still swell; wax coverage, edges and scratches can dominate performance.

Start with the central planning formulation, `ALG_G20_W02`:

- 2.0 g sodium alginate and 100 g water.
- 0.4 g glycerol, nominally pure; record actual product concentration.
- A separate fresh bath: 2.0 g food-grade **calcium lactate pentahydrate** plus 98 g water.
- 0.2 g beeswax **coating feed**, nominally shared between both faces and edges of a planned 10 × 10 cm sheet.

The nine plans vary glycerol to 0.2/0.4/0.6 g and wax feed to 0/0.2/0.4 g. The untreated alginate control has 0.4 g glycerol, no calcium bath and no wax. These choices are experimental parameters, not a reproduced or optimized literature recipe. If the calcium salt's label does not identify its hydration state, do not interpret the modeled calcium inventory as the weighed product's inventory.

1. Hydrate the alginate in the measured room-temperature water with stirring; add glycerol after the mixture is uniform. Record hydration time and visible lumps. Use a level, non-stick **rimmed mold with at least 15 mm retaining depth** for the planned 10 × 10 cm area: this roughly 100 mL wet batch initially makes a layer about 1 cm deep. A flat backing will not retain it, and ambient drying may be slow or uneven. This comparator has no demonstrated easy-processing advantage. Record any unused or spilled mixture. If using a smaller aliquot or larger mold, record its actual mass and area; the whole-batch feed and coating-area budgets no longer describe that specimen directly. This cold preparation is an experimental simplification, not a reproduction of the heated, mechanically mixed laboratory papers.
2. Dry with airflow at room temperature until it can be handled and successive masses have stabilized under the recorded conditions. Record elapsed time, humidity if available and thickness at five points. Ambient drying is not proof of an anhydrous film. If it stays tacky, cracks or cannot be lifted, record a forming failure.
3. For treated variants, keep the film supported and trial brief contact with the fresh calcium-lactate solution. A **one-minute first exposure** is a proposed exploration setting, not a literature-validated cure. Record actual time, uptake, swelling and film loss. Stop if it cannot be handled; do not infer complete crosslinking from bath concentration or time. Skip this step for the untreated control. Rinse handled treated specimens briefly with clean water, recording any further damage, and dry again before coating.
4. For wax variants, follow the selected beeswax supplier's handling guidance. Soften a small amount using a vessel standing in hot water, using the lowest temperature that permits flow and a **maximum bath temperature of 80°C or the supplier's lower limit**. The 80°C water-bath precedent comes from the retrieved laboratory oleogel paper; it is a proposed ceiling for this trial, not validation of a pure-wax coating method. Use indirect warming, a thermometer and tools; do not heat wax over a flame or leave it heating unattended. Record actual bath and wax temperatures. Stop if the wax cannot be applied within this route. Apply a thin coating to both faces and edges, then cool fully before handling. Planned wax feed is not retained wax: weigh the conditioned film before and after coating and record transfer losses, coverage and cracks. A discontinuous coating is a process result, not a successful waterproof film.
5. Test coated and uncoated films alike. All have the same unmeasured environmental-degradation status; natural ingredients do not certify the finished multilayer article.

This protocol does not use calcium chloride, organic solvents, reactive resin curing agents or polymer synthesis. Its reported process limits belong to these small prototypes and do not establish a safe industrial scale-up.

## Water tests that decide the next material choice

Prepare at least **three independently formed specimens per tested formulation**, using fresh alginate baths. If specimens share a preparation batch, label them as shared-batch technical repeats; they do not demonstrate independent batch reproducibility. Use matching geometry and measured thickness when comparing formulations. A thicker PCL cup and a thin alginate film cannot establish a formulation advantage without acknowledging their different constructions.

**Containment:** Fill a PCL vessel to a measured 5 cm head above its bottom. Put it above a dry catch vessel/tray and record start/end water and catch masses for 24 hours at 20–25°C. Protect the catch vessel from evaporation as far as possible. Run a matching evaporation/control setup and record the balance resolution. Look for seams, pinholes and wet surfaces before and after the test. Report raw catch/blank readings, visible drops, test conditions and detection limits. “No detectable leakage in this 24-hour test” is a valid bounded observation; “waterproof under all conditions” is not.

For a flat film, use a supported test frame with a repeatable seal and the same head/temperature/duration. Validate the frame with an impermeable reference sheet before interpreting a film failure; leakage at the clamp is different from leakage through the material. If that fixture is unavailable, perform the immersion test only and leave the containment result blank. Immersion survival does not establish water containment.

**Immersion:** Weigh a conditioned coupon, measure its length/width/thickness, then immerse it in plain room-temperature water for 24 hours. Blot using the same method each time and weigh immediately. Record fragmentation, tackiness, swelling and ability to handle it wet. Redry under the original recorded conditions and weigh again. Calculate:

```text
apparent wet mass gain [%] = 100 (m_blotted_wet - m_initial) / m_initial
apparent redried mass loss [%] = 100 (m_initial - m_redried) / m_initial
```

Both are observations with moisture/blotting/scale uncertainty. Plasticizer or salt leaching can lower mass while water uptake raises it; these values alone do not separate those mechanisms. Keep raw masses. Use specimens large enough for their mass changes to exceed the balance resolution.

**Handling damage:** After the initial test, document ten gentle bending cycles around the same recorded radius, where the specimen can bend without immediately failing. Inspect coating/edges and repeat the containment test. Record a failed bend or coating fracture rather than excluding it. For a rigid cup, use a repeatable handling/deformation test that its geometry supports; do not pretend its stress is the same as a thin-film bend.

A candidate advances only after its actual process is manageable and its relevant water test succeeds with intact wet structure. This study specifies no predicted pass rate, material-property threshold or automated winner. Add tolerable swelling/strength limits for a real application before making a product recommendation.

## Biodegradation is a separate question

Choose the intended receiving environment explicitly: home compost, industrial compost, soil or water. Evidence from one cannot guarantee another. Published PCL work includes controlled powder respirometry and soil weight-loss tests; specimen size, temperature, microbes, crystallinity and blend processing differ from these parts. A supplier assertion without a relevant product/geometry certificate does not certify home compostability.

Home observations of small, retrievable samples can record cracking, visible disintegration and mass change. Recover fragments and keep the material out of drains/natural water. Those observations cannot prove that the entire polymer has mineralized; starch/glycerol leaching and fragment loss can mimic degradation. A relevant validated certification or controlled CO₂/O₂ biodegradation test is needed for that claim. This protocol supplies no decomposition time or guaranteed disposal route.
