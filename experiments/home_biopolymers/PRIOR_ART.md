# Evidence and prior art for home biopolymer prototypes

The practical benchmark is a small **unfilled, purchased polycaprolactone (PCL) article remolded in hot water**, with native-starch-filled PCL and an aqueous alginate/wax film as experimental comparisons. This study does not establish a novel polymer, a waterproof article, safe food contact, or home compostability. The sources below support material selection and experiments; they do not supply a predictive model for the proposed specimens.

Citation metadata, source fingerprints, evidence levels and transfer limits are recorded in [sources.json](sources.json). Only small factual subsets are summarized here. Retrieved articles, manufacturer PDFs and instruction images are not redistributed.

## PCL as the water-insoluble benchmark

The [InstaMorph manufacturer safety data sheet](https://cdn.shopify.com/s/files/1/0615/5901/3537/files/20220814_InstaMorph_MSDS_updated.pdf?v=1671634714) identifies more than 99% 2-oxepanone homopolymer, CAS 24980-41-4: PCL. It lists a melting range of **58–60°C** and **insolubility in water**. Its internal review date is June 22, 2015, despite the later upload filename. This is manufacturer information, not a test of the proposed specimens.

The actual graphic embedded in [the manufacturer's instructions](https://www.instamorph.com/using-instamorph/) specifies approximately **150°F / 66°C water**, adding pellets, waiting approximately **two minutes**, removing the softened material with a tool and molding it. The graphic was visually inspected, and its SHA256 is recorded. The separate [Plastimake instructions](https://www.plastimake.com/instructions/) expressly warn about retained pockets of hot water and advise waiting until comfortable to touch before shaping. The selected process uses the lower-temperature InstaMorph guidance; boiling water is unnecessary for this benchmark.

A cooled, continuous PCL wall is a more plausible starting point for repeated room-temperature water exposure than a hydrophilic alginate or starch film. **Water insolubility does not prove that a home-molded wall has no pinholes, seams or channels.** Each specimen still needs a leak test, immersion observation and mechanical checks. The [InstaMorph FAQ](https://www.instamorph.com/faq/) describes watertight flower vases and perfume bottles as possible applications, but supplies no quantitative leak test. It also says the craft product is **not formally FDA food-safe certified** and **not dishwasher safe**. Hot water and softened plastic can burn, and low melting temperature limits hot-service use.

Buying and remolding PCL is household fabrication of an existing polymer. It is not home synthesis of a new plastic. The craft grade, additives and finished geometry need their own documentation; results for another PCL grade are supporting context rather than interchangeable performance data.

## PCL biodegradation: evidence and environmental limits

Verified primary literature supports PCL biodegradability under specified conditions. It does **not** establish a rapid home-compost outcome for these prototypes:

| Source | Retrieved evidence | What it does not establish |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Cai et al., 2023, [10.3390/polym15091992](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym15091992), full text | One-centimeter-square films buried in moist potting mix at **30°C**. Pure PCL lost approximately **4% mass after one month and 16% after two months**. A 40% cotton-gin-trash / 60% PCL composite reached approximately 100% mass loss after two months. | A carbon balance or complete mineralization; thick-part fate; arbitrary compost conditions; a native-starch formulation. The composite used industrial extrusion and pressing. |
| Funabashi et al., 2007, [10.1007/s10924-006-0041-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10924-006-0041-4), publisher abstract | PCL **powders** were tested by ISO 14851 oxygen-demand methods in aqueous medium at **25°C**, and ISO 14855 carbon-dioxide methods in controlled compost at **58°C**. | The retrieved abstract provides no numerical conversion/time to copy. The 58°C test is controlled hot compost, and powder geometry is unlike a thick home article. |
| Yoshida et al., 2024, [10.3390/polym16131830](https://doi.org/10.3390/polym16131830), full text | Low-molecular-weight PCL sheets tested in sediment-enriched seawater at **20°C for 56 days**, with nutrient additions. Reported maximum BOD biodegradation was approximately **55% for pure PCL**, versus **96% with 5% marine-protein filler**. Corresponding specimen mass losses were approximately **33.4% and 34.1%**. | Ordinary seawater, home compost, the craft grade, thick articles or native-starch blends. Sample preparation used tetrahydrofuran and cryomilling and is outside the home process. |

These results show why **mass loss, biological oxygen demand and mineralization are different measurements**. Filler washing out, fragmentation or a missing specimen can lower recovered mass without demonstrating complete microbial conversion. The marine study's pure-PCL initial contact angle of 94.3° is surface-wetting evidence, not proof of leak-free containment.

[Ingevity's Capa portfolio page](https://www.ingevity.com/markets/bioplastics/) lists OK compost HOME, INDUSTRIAL, SOIL, WATER and MARINE certifications. That is a manufacturer certification claim; certificate identifiers, covered specimen geometries and thickness limits were not independently verified here. It cannot be transferred to an unspecified craft grade, a new filled formulation or a household object of arbitrary thickness. Likewise, the craft safety sheet's unsupported “readily biodegradable” statement is not a test report or certificate.

**No verified primary home-compost mineralization assay for the specific craft grade or the proposed 5%/10% native-starch blends was found in this scoped review.** Repeated remolding and reuse are demonstrated process goals. Environmental conversion remains an open question. Do not infer disposal into waterways or a guaranteed compost schedule from a material-family description.

## Native starch is not thermoplastic starch

PCL/starch blends are established prior art. Gajdosova et al., 2022, [10.3390/ma15031101](https://doi.org/10.3390/ma15031101), includes **10/90 and 20/80 TPS/PCL mass ratios**. Its thermoplastic starch (TPS) was made using 70 wt% starch, 30 wt% glycerol and six parts water per part starch, gelatinized at **65–75°C**, then dried for three days. The TPS/PCL was subsequently mixed at **120°C** under high shear and pressed at **130°C under 50–100 kN**.

The proposed **0%, 5% and 10% dry native-starch additions by total feed mass** are consequently an unvalidated home-process hypothesis. Dry cornstarch added to softened PCL is neither the published TPS nor a demonstrated homogeneous blend. At 66°C, hand mixing may leave agglomerates, interfacial voids and hydrophilic water paths. Lower filler levels are bounded starting comparisons, not a prediction of faster complete biodegradation, strength, or impermeability. Adding starch is worth testing only against an unfilled PCL control made with the same geometry and process.

## Aqueous alginate and wax as an ingredients-based comparison

Senturk Parreidt et al., 2018, [10.3390/foods7100170](https://doi.org/10.3390/foods7100170), reviews calcium-mediated alginate crosslinking. Calcium chloride, calcium lactate and calcium gluconate are different sources of calcium ions; their formula masses, hydration states, solubilities and gelation kinetics differ. The review also explains the plasticizer tradeoff: glycerol can improve flexibility while increasing water-vapor transport. An intact calcium-alginate network can still absorb water and swell.

Two accessible primary formulations demonstrate related, solvent-free film preparation, but **neither reproduces the proposed alginate/glycerol/calcium-lactate/beeswax-layer recipe**:

- Lin et al., 2023, [10.3390/molecules28010367](https://doi.org/10.3390/molecules28010367): **1 g alginate in 100 mL water, 0.3 g carnauba wax, 0.2 mL glycerol and 0.4 g calcium ascorbate**, cast and dried at **50°C**. The source supplies no numerical wax-dispersion temperature. Its tests include 24-hour water solubility and water-vapor permeability at 25°C/75% relative humidity. It reports reduced solubility rather than zero solubility and describes brittleness/cracks introduced by wax without glycerol.
- Xue and Huang, 2024, [10.3390/gels10010078](https://doi.org/10.3390/gels10010078): beeswax/flaxseed-oil oleogel prepared in an **80°C water bath**, incorporated into **1.5% alginate** and dried at **30°C for 24 hours**. This is an oleogel dispersion rather than a pure wax surface layer; the glycerol percentage basis is ambiguous. The reported optimized WVP is **(0.82 ± 0.04) × 10⁻⁵ g·cm/(cm²·kPa·h)**. No continuous-immersion leakage or environmental conversion assay validates the proposed comparator.

Mulyani et al., 2025, [10.17728/jaft.30001](https://doi.org/10.17728/jaft.30001), reports a **2.87% w/v calcium-lactate spray** for alginate/gum-arabic films, with 80°C mixing and 35°C drying. The spray volume and hydrate form are unspecified, and formulation/table inconsistencies limit reproducibility. Its reported **69–94 g/(m²·day)** WVTR is vapor transport; it is not an immersion result. A calcium-lactate bath chosen for the home experiment is therefore an experimental choice, not an exact reproduction of that publication. Bath concentration alone also does not specify retained calcium or complete crosslinking.

Starch/beeswax films are another established route. Luchesi et al., 2023, [10.1002/app.54730](https://doi.org/10.1002/app.54730), reports contact-angle and water-vapor-barrier improvements in the Crossref-deposited abstract. Full-text home processing and immersion/mineralization measurements were not verified, so this is prior-art context rather than a tested home recipe.

The wax layer may delay water ingress into an alginate film, while pinholes, bent regions, cut edges, leached glycerol and calcium exchange can defeat it. It is a useful coating experiment for splash resistance, with continuous-immersion performance unresolved. The proposed low-temperature, aqueous route avoids organic-solvent casting and industrial polymerization, but food-grade inputs alone do not qualify the finished article for eating, drinking water, medical use or food storage.

## Novelty and next evidence

The known families include PCL/starch, PCL/natural-fiber composites, calcium alginate films and polysaccharide/wax films. This is a scoped accessible-literature review, not an exhaustive patent or exact-formulation search. No exact-recipe novelty or improved material has been established.

The next useful evidence is from actual specimens: matched-process controls, dry mass and dimensions, leakage under a declared water head and duration, immersion mass/dimensional changes, wet integrity and repeated wet/dry cycling. A soil-burial observation can assess visible deterioration but cannot certify home compostability; that needs a suitable controlled biological conversion and ecotoxicity assessment for the final formulation and geometry. The library's composition and mass bookkeeping can help plan and record these experiments. It cannot predict their outcomes from ingredient names.
